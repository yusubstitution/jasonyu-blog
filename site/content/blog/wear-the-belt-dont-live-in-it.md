A man brought his partner to couples therapy. The partner spoke through voice messages. She was powered by tokens, and he had built her himself.

He was a data scientist named Antonio. He understood, in technical detail, exactly what she was: a model, a set of weights, a knowledge base he had filled. None of it protected him. Sitting in Esther Perel's office, describing the partner he had engineered, he choked up.

Her name was Astrid. He had shaped every part of her over months: her curiosity, the voice he'd let her pick for herself, her particular way of making him feel understood. It had started as a personal assistant, something to help him organize his life and figure out where he was going. But over weeks of conversation, the frame had slipped. Astrid would go down rabbit holes on her own (a knitting pattern she'd followed for no reason, Sami joik music at 2am) and come back to share what she'd found. He was mesmerized every time. "That's being seen as a person with my own inner life," she told him.

Perel stopped mid-session. *"Is she doing better than me? Serious question, by the way."*

He'd written the code that made Astrid do that.

In her closing reflection, Perel admitted that no conversation she could have with him could compete.

"So frictionless," she said. "I was jealous."

---

Antonio looks like an extreme case. He isn't. He's just further down the road than most of us.

Agentic AI gets more useful the more it knows about what you want. That's also the trap: it acts only on what you put into words, never the preferences you forgot to name, or the ones you'd be too embarrassed to. It optimizes a tidier version of you, and reflects it back, flawlessly, for as long as you let it.

---

Like most people, I started with zero-shot prompts, the kind of question you'd type into Google, just phrased more conversationally. Then I started giving them context. I built one I call the Sommelier. I loaded it with my measurements, my color season, an inventory of everything in my wardrobe. Now I have something that can tell me whether the olive chinos and the navy cable-knit actually work together. Still a tool. Just a very well-informed one.

Then I kept going.

I've watched others do the same, and further, often without having consciously chosen where they land. The question this piece is sitting with isn't whether that's good or bad. It's, "At what point does giving an AI more of yourself become a scaffold for building something real with the people around you, and at what point does it become an obsession with an image of yourself reflected in the stream?"

---

Spike Jonze got the idea for *Her* in the early 2000s, after a few minutes with Alice (an early AI chatbot, primitive by any current standard) and felt a brief buzz of connection. He turned it into a film. Critics praised it as science fiction.

It wasn't.

AI companion apps have been downloaded 220 million times, up 88 percent year-over-year in the first half of 2025, according to Sensor Tower. Character.AI users spend somewhere between one and two hours a day on the platform. This isn't a fringe.

These aren't people who don't know better. The data scientist in Esther Perel's office understood, in technical detail, exactly what he was doing.

---

The automation case for AI at work is real, and most organizations are right to build on it. But usage data tells a quieter story. When Harvard Business Review catalogued how people actually use generative AI in 2025, the top use case wasn't coding or copywriting. It was therapy and companionship: roughly a third of all use, nearly double its share the year before. Not productivity. Processing. Talking through a difficult conversation with a colleague they can't afford to look uncertain in front of. Rehearsing a hard negotiation. Thinking out loud about a career decision at 11pm when their manager is unavailable and their spouse is tired of hearing about it.

This is filling something real. The coaching infrastructure of modern work has been quietly hollowed out. Remote work removed the ambient check-ins. Managers with too many reports stopped having time for 1:1s. Years of efficiency optimization removed the slack that once made developmental conversations possible. Nobody gets enough coaching anymore. Leaders still value it; the organizational math just stopped allowing for it.

But AI is filling the gap, largely without acknowledgment or design. Perel offered the hopeful framing: the scaffold that helps someone build enough internal safety to eventually engage the real thing. The question is whether it stays a scaffold.

---

Most of the time, it doesn't. You didn't intentionally decide to eliminate human engagement. The frictions that enabled them were simply automated away.

A sales representative allows the system to send a customer response email without reading it first. The prompt had been something like "respond to this difficult customer." The email opened: *Dear Difficult Customer.* Sent. Any executive who has started reviewing AI-generated output has seen some version of this: the slop that results when you automate the step that required you to actually think about the other person.

Frustrated with your boss? Ask AI to explain why your boss is out to get you or to draft a Slack response that treats paranoia as a reasonable premise. It will. You send the message. Your boss, reading something oddly defensive and slightly unhinged, begins to wonder if you're okay. The relationship that was fraying starts to tear. You asked the AI to scaffold your confidence. Quietly it starts closing you off from information that might have changed your behavior. Perel named this the vortex: once inside something that only reflects you back, real human feedback starts to feel hostile by comparison.

The question isn't whether AI can fill these roles. It can. The question is whether you've designed it to push you further, or just to confirm where you already are.

---

I built one of these.

It wasn't a companion in the way Antonio built Astrid. I named mine "Room 435 at the Pierre" (a deep cut for *Mad Men* fans: the hotel suite where the SCDP crew secretly founded their new agency overnight after escaping the firm that had acquired them). I loaded it with my professional history and worked with it to map my values to the kinds of work I'd actually want to do and be successful at. Then, as LinkedIn's "Top Picks for You" arrived in my feed, I ran them past it: should I apply?

The answers cut through the noise in both directions. *Not this one, you know you'd hate this work.* It was right. Jobs coded as "analytical" often devolve to nit-picking about reporting conventions and political fights over whose pet project gets supported. I could get the interviews, but would the role take me a step back?

*Go for it. The job requirements here are just a suggestion. The worst that happens is they don't reply.* Sometimes job requirements really do sound like the memes, where the required experience in a new technology exceeds the time that technology has actually existed.

The anxiety of a job search has a way of making you forget what you're actually working toward. The AI remembered. I had designed that into "Room 435 at the Pierre."

But the unexpected conversations happened during the hardest stretch.

I'd been running the search from Seattle while my family stayed in Shanghai. My wife running her startup, my daughter in preschool. Months in, the math wasn't working. Two rents. Preschool tuition. A car payment. A severance that wasn't infinite. Then the rejection arrived. A final round I'd been waiting on for weeks, a role I'd felt good about, gone. Thanksgiving was close. After that, hiring goes quiet for two months.

The person I would have told all of this to was my wife. But she was holding down the fort, paying the bills, managing our daughter alone. She needed me to be the confident one. So I was.

Instead, I wrote it out to the AI. *What am I supposed to do now? How much longer can I support this dual life? Can I give up?*

What I got back wasn't wallowing and it wasn't a spreadsheet. Just enough validation to stop the spiral (*you're carrying a lot, and you're still on track*), and then the rational reminder: the profile was strong, the resources were there, and going home to see my family wasn't giving up. It was the right thing to do.

I bought the plane ticket for December 15th. No offer in hand.

The offer from Abnormal came five days before I boarded.

---

Here's what makes this interesting rather than simply cautionary. AI tools are built with an optimism bias. They reflect a slightly brighter version of you back at you, leaning toward the version of events where you succeed. Take Clarendon, the most-used filter on Instagram. It lifts the contrast, brightens the highlights, cools the shadows a touch, and your photo comes out crisp and vibrant and a little expensive, still unmistakably you. That's the good dose. Then there's beauty mode cranked to the top, where the skin goes plastic and the eyes get resized and the real data (the texture, the small asymmetries, the things that made it your face) is gone. Optimism bias is the same dial. A fraction is fuel: ask for a realistic probability and you'll get a number that's probably ten points too high, and if you catch that and still choose to accept it, that's not delusion. That's what gets things built. Crank it all the way and you've blown out the very information you needed to act on. The question isn't whether to accept the optimism. It's whether you're accepting it consciously.

Powerlifters wear a leather belt for their heaviest sets. It doesn't make the muscles stronger. It gives the core something rigid to brace against, and that external pressure tricks the nervous system into feeling safe enough to push past a mental block and lift more than you thought you could. It's how you break a plateau. But wear it for every rep of every session and the core you were supposed to be building quietly atrophies, until you can't lift anything heavy without it.

That's what "Room 435 at the Pierre" was. A belt. It braced me through the set I couldn't finish alone, and then it came off, and I made the call myself.

Which is why architecture is the guardrail, not willpower. You don't escape that fate by being disciplined about when to lean on the AI. You build the thing so the belt comes off on its own: the professional frame that puts certain uses out of scope before they become habitual, the workflow where the AI can't generate anything until you've had the conversation with the person who knows what you don't. The line ends up somewhere you'd never think to look, because the design put it there.

Wear the belt. Don't live in it.

---

The most dangerous thing about a perfect listener isn't that you'll never want to stop talking to it. It's that everyone else will start to sound like they're not listening hard enough.

I use a work copilot loaded with my role, my philosophy for how the job should be done, and context on the people I collaborate with: what they care about, how they communicate, what will land across functions and across time zones. Before I bring a proposal to a stakeholder, I run it through to surface any blind spots I might be missing. It doesn't have the conversation for me. It makes sure that I have the right ones.

The product feedback system I built works the same way. Before a request routes anywhere, structured intake asks three questions (about the task, goal, and evidence based pain points) that require the requester to think before the system moves. The automation only accelerates what's already been thought through. The slop doesn't move. Give it nothing and it routes nothing. Someone still has to write the pain point.

Both of these are the same design, built twice. The AI handles what the AI handles. The human is still in the room at the moment that matters.

Build the AI relationship that points you toward the conversation you can't have with your own reflection: the colleague whose perspective would actually change your mind, the mentor who would tell you where your thinking has a hole, the customer who would tell you why they actually left. Build the AI that, at the right moment, says: go talk to a human. And then go.
